# Incident Management & Reliability Narratives

Staff and Principal engineers are expected to be calm during the storm. They don't just resolve incidents; they command the room, coordinate teams, and ensure the system learns from the failure.

## 1. Tell me about the worst outage you’ve managed.
*(Adapt this template to your own experience)*

**Context**: In a previous role, our core payment gateway experienced a 45-minute total outage during a high-traffic sales event.
**Impact**: Thousands of failed transactions, resulting in significant potential revenue loss.

**Action (Incident Command)**:
- **Declare & Communicate**: Immediately opened a SEV-1 bridge. Declared myself the Incident Commander (IC). Designated a separate communicator to keep leadership updated every 10 minutes so engineers could focus.
- **Mitigation over Root Cause**: Staff engineers know that restoring service matters more than finding the bug *during* the incident. We noticed the database CPU was pegged at 100%. We didn't investigate *why* a query was bad immediately; we applied a **Circuit Breaker** to stop new traffic, scaled the read replicas horizontally, and gracefully degraded non-essential services (like related-product recommendations).
- **Resolution**: Once traffic was manageable, service was restored.

**Action (Post-Mortem & Remediation)**:
- Led a blameless post-mortem. We discovered a missing index coupled with a retry storm from our frontend clients.
- Implementations: 
  1. Added exponential backoff and jitter to frontend retries.
  2. Implemented strict database query timeouts.
  3. Added an automated chaos engineering test to simulate database saturation.

## 2. How do you build a culture of reliability?
**Narrative**: Reliability isn't a feature you bolt on; it's a culture.

**Action**:
- **SLIs/SLOs**: Shift the conversation from "uptime" to "customer happiness." I implement Service Level Objectives (SLOs) tied to critical user journeys (e.g., 99.9% of "Add to Cart" clicks succeed within 200ms).
- **Error Budgets**: Use error budgets to facilitate the conversation between Product and Engineering. If we burn the budget, we focus on reliability.
- **Blameless Culture**: "You can't fix a system if people are afraid to tell you how it broke." In post-mortems, we ask "What allowed the system to let the human make a mistake?" instead of "Who made the mistake?"
- **Game Days / Chaos Engineering**: We intentionally break things in controlled environments (or prod) to test our monitoring and alerts.

## 3. What do you do when an incident crosses multiple team boundaries?
**Narrative**: Silos kill incident response times.

**Action**:
- Establish a unified observability platform where every team's traces and metrics have a common request ID (Distributed Tracing).
- During the incident, the IC explicitly assigns "Investigate X" to specific SMEs, preventing overlap and confusion.
- Pre-incident: Run cross-team Game Days where teams are forced to debug a simulated issue that spans architectural boundaries.

## 4. Explain the difference between MTTR and MTBF. Which is more important?
- **MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures)**: How often do things break?
- **MTTR (Mean Time To Recovery)**: How fast do we fix them?

**Narrative**: Complex systems *will* fail. Optimizing purely for MTBF leads to stagnation because you never deploy or change anything. At a Staff level, I focus heavily on reducing **MTTR**. 
**Why?**: Fast recovery (via automated rollbacks, blue/green deployments, feature flags) allows developers to iterate quickly without fear. If an error happens, the system detects it and heals itself in 30 seconds. The user barely notices.
